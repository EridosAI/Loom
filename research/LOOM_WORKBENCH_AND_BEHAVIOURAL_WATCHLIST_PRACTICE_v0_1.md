## 1. Purpose

Loom maintains a living research workbench under `research/workbench/` in the repository.

The workbench is used to:

- reconstruct the current active research state;
- index recent experiments, analyses, proposals and unresolved questions;
- preserve provenance across rapid research iterations;
- prevent important observations from being lost between chats;
- maintain the standing Behavioural Development Watchlist.

It is not an independent source of scientific authority.

## 2. Source hierarchy

When reconstructing Loom state, use this hierarchy:

1. current committed canonical repository documents and verified artifacts;
2. `00_LOOM_CURRENT_STATE`;
3. later verified experimental and analysis artifacts;
4. later session retrospectives;
5. earlier retrospectives;
6. historical conversation material.

The Workbench is a living research index and synthesis layer.

It must not silently override higher-authority sources.

Do not silently reconcile contradictions.

Always distinguish:

1. **OBSERVED EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE**
2. **DECISIONS EXPLICITLY ACCEPTED BY JASON**
3. **ASSISTANT / BUILD PROPOSALS**

# 5. Behavioural Development Watchlist

Standing Workbench document:

`research/workbench/30_REVIEWS/BEHAVIOURAL_DEVELOPMENT_WATCHLIST.md`

The watchlist asks:

> **What observable behavioural changes would indicate that lived experience is beginning to matter?**

It is an evolving observational framework.

It is **not**:

- a score;
- a pass/fail criterion;
- a definition of intelligence;
- a required developmental sequence;
- a mechanism specification;
- a canonical statement of what Loom must do.

The list should evolve as Loom produces new evidence.

## 6. Core behavioural families

The watchlist should include, but is not limited to:

### Impact and integrity behaviour

Look for:

- fewer collisions;
- fewer collisions per comparable hazard exposure;
- reduced closing speed;
- reduced peak impulse;
- reduced sustained force;
- reduced damage;
- larger near-miss clearance;
- earlier turning away;
- shorter damaging contact;
- less surface pinning.

### Re-encounter of collision-like circumstances

Ask:

> When an organism later experiences a sensory/proprioceptive context resembling one that previously preceded damage, does its later behaviour differ?

Possible later outcomes include:

- lower collision probability;
- greater clearance;
- reduced closing speed;
- stronger turning away;
- reduced forward drive;
- lower damage.

Similarity measures should preferentially use signals actually available to the organism rather than privileged simulator labels.

### Energy-source behaviour

Look for:

- more productive source encounters;
- more source encounters per unit path/opportunity;
- more distinct sources encountered;
- same-source revisits;
- shorter return time/path;
- longer productive dwell;
- greater energy per contact bout;
- greater energy per contact second;
- fewer brief accidental brushes;
- improved retention near a productive source.

### Revisiting consequential contexts

Distinguish ordinary spatial recurrence from:

- return to previously productive source regions;
- return to restorative regions;
- repeated return after beneficial prior experience.

Do not assume the organism internally represents global location.

### Exploration and spatial organisation

Track:

- coverage;
- revisit fraction;
- displacement/path ratio;
- route diversity;
- repeated loops;
- local trapping;
- coverage per unit effort.

More exploration is not automatically better.

A developing organism may eventually explore less broadly because it begins preferentially returning to useful regions.

### Repair behaviour

After damage, look for:

- shorter time/path to restorative contact;
- greater probability of repair before further damage;
- longer gentle restorative dwell;
- greater repair gained;
- repeated restorative returns;
- more effective low-relative-motion contact.

### Motor organisation

Look for experience-dependent changes in:

- displacement per effort;
- forward/reverse cancellation;
- reversal frequency;
- curvature;
- surface pinning;
- command organisation;
- behaviour beyond the innate spontaneous-motor prior.

### Sensorimotor contingency

Ask whether similar sensory situations increasingly produce systematic action changes.

Examples:

- chemical imbalance followed by turning;
- increasing chemical magnitude followed by locomotor change;
- pre-contact sensory pattern followed by slowing or avoidance;
- post-impact experience followed by later modified responses.

### Behavioural consistency after experience

Repeated consequential experience may produce increasingly consistent responses.

Examples:

- increasingly consistent approach to source-like contexts;
- increasingly consistent avoidance of impact-like contexts;
- increasingly consistent modulation under low-energy conditions.

Consistency itself is not sufficient; relate it to physical consequence.

### Individual developmental phase changes

Preserve rare transitions such as:

- first sustained source revisiting;
- emergence of long productive source dwell;
- transition from impact to avoidance;
- sustained reduction in collision force;
- repair-following-damage behaviour;
- transition from broad roaming to repeated useful routes.

Do not average away individual developmental biographies.

# 7. Opportunity-denominator rule

A standing rule for Loom behavioural analysis is:

> **An apparent behavioural improvement is not interpretable without considering whether the organism had comparable opportunity to display that behaviour.**

Examples:

- fewer collisions must be evaluated relative to hazard exposure;
- source visits relative to source opportunity and/or path travelled;
- softer impacts relative to approach speed and bodily capability;
- repair relative to damaged-state restorative opportunity;
- avoidance relative to comparable pre-hazard contexts;
- source revisiting relative to previous source encounters.

This prevents changes in exposure, immobility, low-energy weakness, or increased random movement from being mistaken for learning.

---

# 8. Link behaviour to internal development

Behavioural observations should be considered alongside internal developmental quantities including:

- associative structure `H`;
- H-use;
- associative return `q`;
- learned E-bank;
- learned I-bank;
- learned E motor/regulatory influence;
- learned I motor/regulatory influence;
- sensory cortical structural development.

Useful questions include:

> Did internal change precede behavioural change?

> Did a behavioural transition occur when learned functional influence became stronger?

> Did repeated consequential experience protect or alter particular associative/sensory structures?

Temporal ordering is useful evidence but does not establish causation.

Causal claims require an appropriate matched branch, omission, ablation or other controlled counterfactual.

---

# 9. Current analytical lesson

Recent Loom work has reinforced the importance of separating:

1. **experience acquisition**
2. **internal learning**
3. **functional expression**
4. **behavioural consequence**

An organism may:

- encounter the world successfully without learning;
- learn internally without expressing that learning strongly;
- express learned influence without producing beneficial behaviour;
- show useful behaviour without the causal mechanism yet being established.

Analyses should preserve these distinctions.

---

# 10. Maintenance of the watchlist

After a substantive Loom result, review the watchlist for:

- newly observed behavioural phenomena;
- stronger or weaker evidence for an existing phenomenon;
- newly discovered confounds;
- improved measurement approaches;
- behavioural measures shown to be misleading or uninformative.

Each substantive update should retain:

- date;
- source artifact;
- observed evidence;
- interpretation;
- whether the item remains unresolved;
- whether causal testing is still required.

Do not retrospectively rewrite earlier experimental conclusions.

---

# 11. Use by future Loom chats

When a new Loom chat begins substantial experimental design or analysis:

1. read `00_LOOM_CURRENT_STATE` first;
2. consult current canonical sources where required;
3. consult the Workbench for recent active research state;
4. consult `BEHAVIOURAL_DEVELOPMENT_WATCHLIST.md` when considering developmental evidence;
5. preserve the source hierarchy;
6. distinguish evidence, Jason decisions, proposals and unresolved interpretation;
7. update the Workbench after major work rather than allowing the research index to drift.

The watchlist should inform what to observe.

It must not constrain Loom into producing behaviours we happened to anticipate.
